Start with a diagnostic

What is brand dysfunction?

Pick up any of these you’ve heard said about your own brand, and we’ll sort them below, from healthy to dysfunctional.

  • “It works. I'm just not sure it's working as hard as it could.”

    Most settled brands sound like this from the inside. Nothing is broken, so nobody is asked to fix it, and the cost shows up as growth that never happened, which doesn't appear on any report.

  • “Our agencies came back with a menu of options and no real point of view.”

    A menu of options hands the decision, and the risk that comes with it, straight back to the client. You hired an agency to take a position and defend it, and a menu usually means nobody on their side was willing to.

  • “Our brand strategy actually comes up when hard decisions get made.”

    This is the best test we know of whether a strategy works. Most strategies get approved, and then the next hard decision gets made without anyone opening them.

  • “We did the positioning work, and now it sits in a deck nobody opens.”

    Usually the problem is what happened in the approval rounds. Each stakeholder sands off the part they object to, and what survives is too general to settle any decision, so people stop reaching for it.

  • “We know most launches miss, but our batting average feels well below normal.”

    When launches keep missing, we'd look at the process before the products. In most companies each launch is briefed from scratch, sold in on its own promise, and judged on a few months of data, before buyers have had time to form a habit.

  • “Customers describe us the way we'd describe ourselves.”

    It means what you promise and what people experience match, and most brands can't say that. Keep checking anyway, because the two drift apart a little at a time, and customers usually notice the gap before anyone inside does.

  • “Nobody loves our packaging, but every redesign ends up only slightly better.”

    Redesigns judged on objections end up safe. Each round fixes whatever people complained about in the last one, and nobody in the process is asked whether a shopper would reach for it.

  • “Ask our leadership what the brand stands for and you'll get five different answers.”

    Each of those answers is probably right about its own part of the business. The trouble is that nobody has committed to one theory of the brand that sits above them, so every function pulls it toward what that function needs.

  • “Our agency brought us one clear recommendation, and we backed it.”

    One recommendation with an argument behind it is worth more than any number of options, because someone had to rule the others out and can tell you why. Backing it is the client's half of the deal.

  • “We're still coasting on what made us successful years ago.”

    What made you successful was shaped for the market as it was then. It still feels comfortable, and that comfort is what makes it hard to see how far the market has moved.

  • “What we promise and what customers actually get have drifted badly apart.”

    Drift starts when nobody checks the strategy against what customers get. Writing a better promise won't close the gap, because the gap is in the experience.

  • “Our leadership tells the same story about the brand, without a script.”

    Few leadership teams can do this. When they can, decisions move faster, because nobody has to stop and work out what the brand would do.

  • “Every campaign starts from scratch, so we keep relitigating the basics.”

    Every brief reopens the same questions when there's no agreed theory of the brand to settle them. The team ends up spending its best hours on arguments it has already had.

  • “We're so close to it that we can't see what's wrong anymore.”

    Every company gets this way eventually. People inside share the same assumptions, and you can't question an assumption you don't know you're making (which is most of what an outsider is for).

  • “When the market told us something, we changed course, and it worked.”

    Acting in order to learn is a habit, and you have it. Most teams wait to be certain first, and in a market that keeps moving, certainty never comes.

  • “Nobody complains about the brand, and nobody gets excited about it either.”

    Indifference is the hardest kind of settling to spot, because nothing about it generates a complaint. It also gets more expensive every year, since people who don't care about a brand switch the first time a cheaper one is on the shelf.

  • “We've rebranded twice in five years, and nothing really changed.”

    A rebrand changes how the brand looks and leaves the people, briefs and approvals that produced the old one in place. Within a year or two, they produce the same brand again.

  • “Our packaging does real work on shelf, and we can tell you why.”

    Plenty of packaging works without anyone knowing why. Knowing why means that when it's time for the next change, you'll know which parts to protect.

Most brands sit somewhere on a line.

Here's how we see it. Brand dysfunction is the gap between what a brand is supposed to do for the business and what it actually does. It opens slowly, through drift, and it usually goes unnoticed until something outside the company – a new competitor, a change in how people shop, a retailer handing your shelf space to its own label – shows how wide it has become.

The people are usually not the problem. Most brand teams are smart and hardworking, and the tools they use work. But brand work has to pass through the rest of the organization before it changes anything, and that's where it tends to get stuck.

So it helps to think of brand health as a position on a line. It runs from healthy, through settled, to dysfunctional, and brands drift along it without anyone deciding to move. The same brand can be healthy in one market and settled in another, which is part of why the drift is so hard to spot.

Work goes where the structure sends it.

Every organization has a structure that decides how work moves: planning cycles, agency rosters, approval chains, quarterly targets. Capable people follow those paths, and the paths produce the outcome. If the structure pushes every brief toward consensus and every launch toward the safe option, that's what the brand will get, no matter who's in the room.

Most of the limits were never decided. They were inherited: the category's conventions, the channel mix, the way launches have always been done. Over time they stop looking like choices and start looking like physics. Some are real, but a surprising number are habits nobody has questioned (usually because questioning them wasn't anyone's job).

It's also hard to see from inside. A company that mostly talks to itself describes itself in its own language and tests ideas against its own assumptions, so every round of internal refinement feels like progress while moving the thinking further from how the market sees the business. Kodak is the famous case. One of its own engineers built the first digital camera in 1975, and the company kept betting on film until it filed for bankruptcy in 2012. An outside view helps for a simple reason: it doesn't share the assumptions, so it can see which ones are doing the damage.

So small fixes bounce off. Brand work done inside the same structure gets absorbed by it. It's negotiated down to something nobody objects to, which also means nobody is passionate about it, and a year later the business is behaving exactly as it did before.

Settling is the easy option.

When a brand is working, the status quo is exactly what it should protect, and we'll say so. But the status quo is also the easy option. Nobody has to argue for it or gets blamed for it, and staying put feels safe even when it quietly costs more than moving would (Kahneman and Tversky called this loss aversion: a loss weighs more than a gain of the same size, and the gains from moving are the ones nobody can see yet).

But most teams know when they're settling. Ask a marketing leader whether they're happy with their brand, and the length of the pause tells you most of what you need to know.

How a brand gets functional again.

Take the rules apart. We break the category's conventions into their pieces and test which ones hold. Then we rebuild what survives into a position only this business could occupy, one that makes complete sense to your team and is hard to copy for a competitor still following the old rules.

Commit to a theory. We say plainly what's happening, why, and what would have to change for a different outcome, including what we don't know and what would prove us wrong. A menu of options leaves the client holding the risk. A theory takes a position and shows its work, so you can argue with it.

Act to learn. You can't collect every answer before you move, and waiting for certainty is just a more respectable way of settling. So we pick a few first moves small enough to try soon and clear enough to read, and let the market show how it really responds. Each result sharpens the theory.

Brands rarely fail all at once.

They drift, one reasonable decision at a time, and drift can be corrected. Nobody controls a brand outright, since its meaning gets made together with the people who buy it. But most of the limits a team works inside were chosen at some point, and what was chosen once can be chosen again, on purpose this time.
