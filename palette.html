01 — COLOR

Brand guidelines / revision

Two inks,
one meeting point.

The palette is sampled from a single reference image: two saturated blots on a cool paper ground, touching at exactly one place. That structure is the system — red carries the argument, blue carries the structure, and they are allowed to meet once.

01.4

Inverted band — ink

The accent has to survive the flip.

Red 500 is too heavy against ink, so dark sections step up to red 400 for interface elements and red 300 for accent text. The blue does the opposite — it lightens to 300 so the plotted line stays legible.

01.5

Inverted band — deep blue

Same weight. Different air.

Deep blue and ink sit at effectively the same luminance, so alternating between them changes the temperature of a section without changing its contrast. Use ink for work and proof; use deep blue for philosophy and contact.

01.6

Rules of use

  1. Red and blue meet once per page. On the reference image the two blots touch at a single bleed. Full-strength red and full-strength blue may sit adjacent in exactly one place per page — normally the hero plot. Everywhere else, one of them is reduced to a grid, a hairline, or a coordinate label.
  2. Red argues. Blue structures. Red marks the claim: the outcome, the figure, the call to action. Blue marks the frame: axes, numbering, grid, dark ground. If an element is neither a claim nor a frame, it stays neutral.
  3. Never distinguish data by red versus blue alone. The two anchors are 1.24:1 against each other and read as one value in grayscale. Plotted curves must also differ in weight, dash pattern, or direct label.
  4. No warm grays, no pure black, no pure white on ground. All neutrals carry the paper's cool cast. White is reserved for text sitting on the accent.
  5. The accent stays scarce. Same discipline as the type system: red appears at most twice per viewport. Frequency is what makes it read as signal rather than decoration.
  6. Punctuation takes the color of the type before it. A trailing mark — period, comma, closing parenthesis, colon — belongs to the word or phrase it closes, not to the paragraph's default color. If a red clause ends in a period, the period is red too. Splitting the color at a tag boundary (<em>Growth</em>. instead of <em>Growth.</em>) reads as a rendering bug, not a choice — keep closing punctuation inside the colored span.
