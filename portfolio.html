Portfolios and brand architecture

Portfolios are built one good decision at a time. Each brand, sub-brand, and product was launched or acquired for a reason, and most of them still earn their keep.

But over time, the reasons blur. In a house of brands, sibling brands start competing for the same budget and the same shopper. Under a single masterbrand, the name gets stretched across so many products and variants that it's no longer clear what it stands for. Either way, the architecture stops matching how people actually shop the category, and the portfolio ends up working against itself in ways no one planned.

How to recognize it
Two sibling brands are chasing the same shopper, or nobody can agree on what to call the next product.

Nobody owns the portfolio

Each brand team is measured on its own results, so each one does the sensible thing for its own brand. The question of what the portfolio as a whole should do has no natural owner, and it gets answered by default in annual planning. It usually takes an outside change, like a retailer resetting the shelf or a competitor moving into the gap between two brands, to show how much the portfolio has drifted.

Shoppers won't sort it out on their own. They move through a crowded aisle more or less on autopilot, and they give the shelf a split second to understand why a product exists and why they should choose it. An architecture that only makes sense on the org chart asks them to decode it, and most won't bother.

Here's a simple test. If you had to cut one brand's budget in half tomorrow, would the leadership team agree on which one?

What each brand and product is for

Find the epicenter.

We start with what the brand is famous for, whether that's a single product or a feeling, and work outward through every range, sub-brand, and variant, asking how far each one sits from that center in product, consumer, occasion, and price. The shape of the map says a lot. A brand huddled around its core may be struggling to extend, and one spread across everything has probably over-extended.

Give each brand a role.

We decide each brand's job and what it's allowed to do that its siblings aren't. That includes the parent brand, since some portfolios need a strong masterbrand and others are better served by one that stays quiet. Products under one name need to be the same but different: distinct enough to explain why each exists, and consistent enough that the name keeps its meaning. Overlap is sometimes fine, as long as someone decided it.

Build it into planning.

An architecture that isn't used when budgets are set won't change much. We build it into the planning process and the briefs that come out of it, along with a simple way to decide what to call the next product, so it keeps working after we've gone (a lovely portfolio map on a slide has never once changed a media plan).

At Razorfish, John led brand strategy across Church & Dwight's portfolio, from Arm & Hammer and OxiClean to Trojan, Batiste, and First Response. His job was to keep the brand expression coherent across the portfolio so that each brand built its own equity. Over that period the portfolio grew sales by more than $1 billion, and the brands held their #1 or #2 positions in their categories.

His team also overhauled how partner agencies were briefed and managed during annual planning, including oversight of a nine-figure media budget.

Also: at 360i, John led brand and creative strategy for Nestlé Waters' $1.9 billion portfolio of six brands, raising purchase intent 7% and contributing to a 2% increase in penetration and a 2.2% increase in buy rate.
