Positioning that gets used

The re-positioning was a real investment. The research was thorough, the leadership team signed off, and the agencies were briefed the following week.

But six months later, the new positioning lives in a deck on a shared drive, and the brand is making the same decisions it made before anyone wrote it. Nothing has really changed – and sooner or later, the results show it.

How to recognize it
Ask three people on the team to say it from memory, and you'll hear three different versions.

Why positioning ends up in a drawer

Positioning is usually built by consensus, and consensus wears away whatever anyone objected to along the way. What survives tends to be inoffensive – benefit statements that everyone internally loves but no one on the street believes. A brand manager can't use a statement like that to turn down a promotion or a line extension, so it quietly stops being consulted, and the brand starts drifting again.

It also tends to stay inside the communications brief. The positioning shapes the advertising and never reaches pricing, the product, or the pack, which is where shoppers form most of their opinion of a brand. The work fixed how the brand talks, and left the causes of the drift where they were.

Seeing the positioning through

Pick a fight.

A position means something only if it rules something out. We start by naming what the brand is against, whether that's a category convention or a customer frustration nobody has taken seriously. A clear enemy makes the choices downstream much easier.

Test it against real decisions.

We take the draft position into the decisions the team is facing this quarter, like the next promotion or the next agency brief. If it doesn't make at least one of them easier, it isn't finished.

Follow it past the ad.

We trace the positioning through the rest of the business, from pricing and packaging to the customer service script, and stay with it until it holds. The list of places where it breaks tends to matter more than the statement itself.

From the work, before Function

Arm & Hammer Baking Soda2024

A reason to pay a little more for baking soda

After a price increase, Arm & Hammer Baking Soda was losing volume to private labels that shoppers saw as the same product for less. We repositioned it from a pantry staple to a source of hands-on science for kids and parents, and took that position out of the advertising and into classrooms with the first annual Baking Soda Bottle Rocket Day on October 10, 2024.

More than 180,000 students in 14,000 classrooms took part, including over 270 underserved schools, with more than 5,000 launches, the largest baking soda rocket launch on record. Purchase intent rose 4.5 points and brand affinity rose 6.3 points.

Also: we wrote the strategy and brief for Anthem's first digital consumer campaign, which acquired 769,000 customers, three months ahead of a 600,000 annual goal, at prices 5–20% above the market average.

Positioning is only as good as the next decision it makes easier.

If yours isn't making decisions easier yet, something is causing it to drift, and the cause is usually visible to someone outside the building. Finding it is what the diagnostic is for.
